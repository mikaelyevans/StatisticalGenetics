<h1>Literature Review</h1>
<h3>Ancestry</h3>
<p>
Genes provide valuable insights into both visible and hidden traits, making them essential for understanding disease inheritance, physical characteristics, and ancestry. Numerous consumer genetics companies, such as Ancestry.com and 23andMe, offer individuals a glimpse into their health and heritage by analyzing their genomes and comparing them to extensive databases. While there is a strong desire among people to learn more about their ancestry, how is ancestry truly defined?
</p>
<p>
Ancestry is a term that most of us have defined in our own heads through knowledge we have accumulated over our lifetimes, and can be summed up as the origins of a person’s family. The journal article <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1008624">What is Ancestry? (PLOS Genetics)</a> examines this topic in a more scientific context. The authors define genetic ancestors as the “individuals from whom you are biologically descended” and ancestry as “information about [ancestors] and their genetic relationship to you”. In science, ancestry has been simplified by categorizing it into certain groups including race, ethnicity, region, and country, but the truth is that ancestry and inheritance do not fit perfectly into these categories. Dividing people into these different ancestry categories introduces the possibility of discrimination, misrepresentation, and division based on a person's identity. 
</p>
<p>
The article <a href="https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/26902/using-population-descriptors-in-genetics-and-genomics-research-a-new">Using Population Descriptors in Genetics and Genomics Research (National Academies)</a> discusses how it is not useful to use simplified variables to try to explain complex genetic variation in populations. As genetics are becoming more accessible to study, it is important for scientists researching in this field to consider these conversations and their selection of population descriptors.
</p>
<h3>Race and Ethnicity</h3>
<p>
Race and ethnicity are examples of human-made classification systems that have had divisive effects on people for centuries. There is nothing in our genes that specifically “codes” for what race/ethnicity we identify as, but people belonging to the same racial and ethnic groups are likely to share similar genetic makeup. Self-identification of race and ethnicity in surveys, job applications, and more has become integrated into our society and peoples’ sense of self. The journal article <a href="https://www.cell.com/ajhg/fulltext/S0002-9297(14)00476-5?theme=Untitled%2Cmontraykreyol.org%2Farticle%2F%7B-%7D%3Ftheme%2Cmontraykreyol.org%2C0.016772065"> The Genetic Ancestry of African Americans, Latinos, and European Americans Across the United States (American Journal of Human Genetics)</a> discusses the difference between Americans’ <strong>genetic</strong> and <strong>self-reported</strong> ancestries, races, and ethnicities. This paper uses a <strong>Genome Wide Association Study</strong> (GWAS), an analysis of individuals’ entire genome, to group people by geographic origin and compare this to their self-reported identities.
</p>
<p>
The authors ask several main questions: Is it possible to see distinct ancestral groups, or is there a continuous spectrum of ancestral variation in individuals? If there are distinct ancestral clusters, do they accurately represent an individual’s self-identified geographic origin? The study shows that it is possible for genes to accurately predict an individual’s geographic origin, and that ancestry is more complex than individuals usually perceive. 
</p>
<p>
A way to combat this categorization is to instead look at ancestry <strong>geographically</strong>. The scientific journal article <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07331"> Genes Mirror Geography Within Europe (Nature)</a> performed a study that shows this occurrence of genes mirroring geography and suggests that ancestral geography be used as a grouping factor instead of classifications like race and ethnicity. This article calls for an open discussion on how categorizers are selected in scientific research.
</p>

<h3>Population Structure</h3>
<p>
	<strong>Observational data</strong> introduces <strong>confounding variables</strong>, or variables that influence both the dependent variable and predictor of interest. Confounding variables can introduce Type 1 Error, false positive results, and make scientific study statistics inaccurate by <strong>inflating p-values</strong>. Some examples of confounding variables include population structure, familial relationships, and more. Confounding by population structure can be accounted for in genome wide analysis studies by implementing <strong>Principal Component Analysis</strong>. It is not possible to account for confounding variables by removing them from the study since this would instantiate <strong>omitted variable bias</strong> which distorts the relationship between the included variables and biases the results of the study.
</p>
